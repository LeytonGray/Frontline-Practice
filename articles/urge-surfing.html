Urge Surfing

A practical guide to noticing urges and choosing what happens next

Leyton Gray
November 2026

Download printable PDF

What is urge surfing?

An urge or craving can feel as though it needs an immediate response. It may show up as a physical sensation, a thought, an image, an emotion or a strong pull towards doing something. As the urge grows, our attention can narrow, making the relief, pleasure or other benefit we expect from acting on it particularly compelling.

Urge surfing helps us create some space between experiencing an urge and deciding what to do next. Rather than immediately acting on the urge or trying to push it away, we practise noticing it, allowing it to be present and observing what happens.

Urges can build, peak, subside and return, much like waves. They don't always follow a predictable pattern, and some last longer than others. We can learn to respond differently even while an urge remains strong.

Getting to know the wave

Before introducing the exercise, it can help to explore how urges show up for the person.

  • When do urges tend to arise?
  • What's the first sign that an urge is developing?
  • How does it show up in the body, thoughts or behaviour?
  • What does acting on the urge seem to offer?
  • What usually happens next?

These questions can help identify opportunities to respond earlier and understand what the behaviour provides.

Choosing what comes next

A person may still want to act on an urge. Urge surfing offers an opportunity to consider their options, which might include changing their environment, seeking support or taking another practical action.

The three closing questions allow the person to respond according to what is workable in the moment.

  • What would help me through this moment?
  • What do I want to do next?
  • What matters to me here, and bearing that in mind, what do I want to do next?

These questions are invitations, not stages to complete. Values work doesn't need to become another task someone must perform in the middle of a strong urge.

Considerations for practice

Explain the approach beforehand and practise when urges arise naturally, ideally when they feel manageable. There's no need to deliberately recall, imagine or provoke an urge.

Pay attention to how the person responds and adapt the exercise accordingly. They can stop, seek support or use another strategy if attending to the urge becomes overwhelming or unhelpful.

Urge surfing is one option among several. It is not a substitute for appropriate assessment, support or treatment where there are immediate safety or medical concerns.

An important principle for practitioners

What happens after the exercise doesn't erase what happened during it.

Someone may spend a few minutes noticing an urge and exploring their experience, then subsequently act on the urge or stop the exercise early. That doesn't invalidate the effort or any learning that took place.

Our role is to remain curious about the experience and what the person might take from it, without turning the outcome into a judgement about their progress.

Urge surfing was developed by G. Alan Marlatt within relapse prevention.

Guided practice: Riding the Wave

Adapt the script, language and pace according to the person's responses. Choose whichever closing question seems most appropriate. The pauses are suggestions rather than fixed timings.

Getting comfortable

Find a position that feels reasonably comfortable for you. You can sit, stand or move a little if that helps. You can keep your eyes open, soften your gaze or close them if you prefer.

We're going to spend a little time noticing an urge and what happens when we give ourselves some room to experience it.

You don't need to make anything happen, and you can stop whenever you wish.

[Pause]

Noticing the urge

If you're experiencing an urge right now, take a moment to notice how it's showing up for you.

It might be a physical sensation, a thought, an image, a feeling or a pull towards doing something.

Start with whatever you notice.

If you notice something in your body, where do you feel it most strongly?

[Pause]

Getting curious

Take a little time to explore whatever you've noticed.

If it's a physical sensation, what's it like?

Perhaps there's tightness, pressure, warmth, heaviness, movement or restlessness.

You might notice its size, shape, texture or temperature. You might associate it with a colour. Perhaps it moves, pulses or stays still.

Just notice whatever stands out.

[Allow time]

Making room

See whether you can allow the experience to be as it is, just for these next few moments.

You might notice an urge to push it away, distract yourself or make it feel different. That's understandable.

If that happens, see whether you can notice that urge too.

You don't need to make the feeling different.

[Pause]

If it helps, you can rest some of your attention on your breathing, the contact of your feet with the floor, the chair supporting you or the sounds around you.

Noticing what the mind is saying

You might also become aware of thoughts accompanying the urge.

Perhaps your mind is making promises, offering reasons, bargaining with you or telling you that you need to do something about how you're feeling.

See whether you can notice those thoughts as they come and go.

You don't need to argue with them or work out whether they're right.

You might simply notice, there's my mind talking, and return your attention to what you're experiencing.

[Pause]

Watching the wave

Bring your attention back to the urge.

What's happening now?

Has anything changed? Perhaps the sensation has moved, become stronger or weaker, or changed in some other way.

Perhaps it feels much the same.

You may notice the urge rising and falling, or coming in waves. Another wave may follow.

See whether you can continue noticing what's here, without needing it to be different.

[Allow time]

Choosing what comes next

As we bring the exercise towards a close, consider what might be helpful for you.

You might ask yourself:

What would help me through this moment?

Or perhaps:

What do I want to do next?

Or, if it feels useful to think a little more widely:

What matters to me here, and bearing that in mind, what do I want to do next?

Just see whether one of these questions helps you.

[Pause]

When you're ready, bring your attention back to the room. Notice where you are and the space around you.

Take your time.

After the exercise

Practitioner note: Allow the person to describe their experience in their own words. You might begin by asking, “How was that for you?” Explore what they noticed and what they might take from the exercise.
